Simulated worlds

From a robot's first step to the birth of galaxies.

The same idea — rules applied over time — runs a robot arm, a hurricane and an entire universe. What changes is the scale, and the physics engine underneath.

Enter the worlds

Three scales of simulation

Each world lives through the same phases — but at wildly different sizes and speeds.

01

Robotics

Millimetres · milliseconds

Robots are trained and tested in simulation long before they touch the real world. A virtual robot can fall a million times overnight without breaking a single motor — and learn to walk, grasp, or fly from those failures.

  • Simulates: joints, motors, friction, contact, sensors like cameras and lidar
  • Used for: reinforcement learning, motion planning, safety testing
  • Hard part: the sim-to-real gap — reality is messier than the model
  • Tools: MuJoCo, NVIDIA Isaac Sim, Gazebo, PyBullet

02

Environment

Kilometres · decades

Climate and weather models split the planet into a 3D grid of cells and solve the equations of fluid flow, heat and moisture in each one. Ecosystem models add living things — forests, fish, fires — that react to that changing world.

  • Simulates: air and ocean currents, temperature, rain, ice, carbon
  • Used for: forecasts, climate projections, flood and wildfire risk
  • Hard part: chaos — tiny errors grow, so many runs are averaged as an ensemble
  • Tools: CESM, WRF, ECMWF's IFS, agent-based ecosystem models

03

Universe

Megaparsecs · billions of years

Cosmological simulations start with a nearly smooth early universe and let gravity do the rest. Billions of particles of dark matter and gas clump into a cosmic web, then into galaxies, stars and black holes.

  • Simulates: gravity, gas dynamics, star formation, cosmic expansion
  • Used for: testing theories of dark matter and how galaxies form
  • Hard part: every particle pulls on every other — clever tree and mesh methods keep it tractable
  • Tools: GADGET, AREPO (IllustrisTNG), Enzo, the Millennium Simulation

Inside the physics engine

A physics engine is the heart of a simulation: the code that decides how things move, collide and push on each other. Strip away the details and nearly every engine runs the same loop, many times per simulated second.

  1. 1

    Apply forces

    Gravity, motors, wind, springs — add up every force acting on every body.

  2. 2

    Integrate

    Turn forces into new velocities and positions over one small timestep, dt.

  3. 3

    Detect collisions

    A cheap broad phase finds pairs that might touch; a precise narrow phase checks them.

  4. 4

    Solve constraints

    Push overlapping bodies apart, keep joints together, apply friction and bounce.

  5. 5

    Advance time

    Record the new state, move the clock forward by dt — and repeat.

The timestep

Smaller steps are more accurate but slower. Most engines use a fixed step (say 1/240 s) so results are repeatable, and run several steps per rendered frame.

Integrators

Explicit Euler is simple but gains energy and explodes. Semi-implicit Euler and Verlet stay stable for games and robots; Runge–Kutta and symplectic methods serve science.

Rigid, soft & fluid

Rigid bodies never bend — perfect for robot links. Soft bodies deform like cloth or tissue. Fluids are solved on grids or as particles (SPH) for oceans, air and gas clouds.

Determinism

Same inputs, same outputs. Without it you can't reproduce a bug, compare two controllers, or trust that an experiment actually changed what you think it did.

A tiny physics engine, live

This box runs the exact loop above: forces, semi-implicit Euler integration, collision detection and resolution, at a fixed 1/120 s step. Click or tap to drop bodies in.

bodies 0 steps 0 gravity Earth 
// one fixed step of the engine
for (body of bodies) {
  body.v += gravity(body) * dt;   // 1. forces → 2. integrate velocity
  body.p += body.v * dt;          // 2. integrate position
}
for (pair of nearbyPairs(bodies)) // 3. detect
  if (overlaps(pair)) resolve(pair);  // 4. separate + bounce
time += dt;                       // 5. advance
